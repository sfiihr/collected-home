# Project Retrospective

## What Went Well
The strongest part of this project was having a clearly-defined scope before I started building this website.  I knew my project requirements, but I also had a vision for the site itself - interior design-focused, a diary of my personal journey, with colors that reflected the aesthetic I'm leaning into in my home.  Once I began writing, it was easy to tell my story because it is one that is uniquely mine.

I have also found GitHub to be easier to use as I worked through this project.  Creating logical hierarchies started to "click", steps to execute became more natural, and this project became a culmination of the skills I've learned over the last eight weeks.

## What Went Wrong

My biggest challenge was the the biggest risk I identified for this project: time.  For the past several months I've been leading a major M&A project for our Cloud business.  The deal closed this week and, as one of two focals for this transaction, I've somehow been busier than the weeks before.  I underestimated how much time I'd spend working, and also how much time it would take to properly complete this final project.  Even the small tasks took time to ensure syntax was properly executed, the code rendered on the site as I had envisioned, and everything fit seamlessly together.

I've also learned here - though I suppose I've always known - that I will tweak and tweak until I put my own timeline at risk.  This is a personal challenge I face often - grappling with "good enough for me" versus "good enough for the general population."  It was particularly challenging for this project because I am incredibly passionate about the topic of interior design.  It's my creative outlet in what's otherwise a highly technical existence every day, so I tend to get lost in it, and that put my delivery at risk.  It was a great learning for me that I have to continually monitor scope and sometimes prevent myself from being the source of scope creep.

## What I Would Do Differently

If I had to do it over, I would build and publish a basic version of my website earlier in the process.  No styling, simply words, layout, and a feeling for what I'm creating.  It would give me opportunity to digest my canvas and tweak it over time, add those extra design elements, and take my project from "good enough for the general population" to "good enough for me."

I would also aim to stick firmly to the timeline I laid out, even if that made me uncomfortable.  I learned that bits and pieces of a complex project, even one where my skills are still limited, can make a meaningful difference on deadline day.  While events this week were a bit out of my control, committing to even 15 or 30 minutes a day to progress the project would mean that I wouldn't be writing my retrospective 45 minutes ahead of submission.  

Overall, I'm happy with my final website given my skill level and time challenges I had to navigate.  It reflects my scope and the feedback from peer reviews and is a clear representation of me.  More importantly, though, the end to end process for this final project gave me a thorough understanding of how properly scoping, anticipating and planning for risks, executing according to plan, and cleanly closing out projects come together to create a strong finished product.
